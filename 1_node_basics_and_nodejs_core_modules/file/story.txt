The Clock of Little SecondsIn a dusty shop at the edge of town, Silas worked with gears so small they looked like dust on his fingertips. He spent sixty years fixing church bells and pocket watches. He knew the heavy tick of a grandfather clock and the rapid beat of a stop-watch.One rainy Tuesday, a stranger left a plain wooden box on his counter. Inside lay a clock with only one hand, and no numbers on its face. Instead of ticks, it made a soft, warm hum.Silas touched the glass. The single hand did not sweep smoothly. It jumped. It waited for three minutes, then jerked forward a fraction of an inch.When the hand jumped, Silas felt a sudden rush of warmth in his chest. It was the exact feeling he had forty years ago when his daughter first grabbed his thumb with her tiny hand. He blinked, stunned. The clock had not measured time. It had measured a memory.He watched the dial again. Ten minutes later, the hand twitched. A sharp smell of salt water filled the small room, and he was ten years old again, standing on a windy pier with his grandfather, eating a hot potato wrapped in paper.Silas understood. The machine did not count down the minutes left in a day. It collected the quiet, bright seconds of a life that people forgot they had saved. It held the laughter in a kitchen, the smell of rain on dry dust, the exact second a lonely heart realized it was loved.He did not call the stranger back. He set the wooden box on his work desk, turned off his bright overhead lamp, and sat in the glow of the single moving hand, waiting to see what the next second would bring.
